Every studio has a portfolio; the portfolios all look good, nobody shows the failures. The questions below are how you find the failures anyway: not by asking about them directly, but by testing whether the answers are specific, unguarded, and boring in the way that real work is boring. Ask them in order.

## On scope and money

**1. "What is included, line by line?"** Design, copy, development, launch checks, and what happens after. The quote that cannot be itemized is the quote that will be renegotiated. See [what a website costs](/blog/how-much-does-a-website-cost-in-namibia) for the lines to expect.

**2. "Who writes the words?"** The most common project-killer hides here. Copy in scope: the project runs. Copy out of scope and unwritten: the project stalls for months whatever the calendar said.

**3. "Who owns the domain, the hosting, and the files?"** The only acceptable answer is you. Anything else converts a purchase into a subscription you cannot cancel, the expensive pattern documented in [the true cost of a cheap website](/blog/the-true-cost-of-a-cheap-website).

**4. "What happens after launch?"** Bug-fix window, maintenance terms, response times. "We'll be around" is not a term.

## On the work itself

**5. "Walk me through a project like mine."** Not the showcase version, the calendar version: phases, feedback rounds, what you will be asked to provide and when. A studio that cannot narrate its own process has not got one.

**6. "Show me work for a business like mine."** Sector experience matters less than size-and-shape experience: an organization of your complexity, an audience like yours. Our equivalent is [the cases page](/cases), where the shape of each engagement is written down.

**7. "What will you refuse to do?"** The best studios have answers: refusing to launch without redirects, refusing template shortcuts on [performance](/blog/core-web-vitals-explained-for-business-owners), refusing to invent client testimonials. A studio that will do anything has already decided what corners look like.

**8. "How do you handle phones and slow connections?"** If the answer is a pause, the work will be designed on fiber and experienced on prepaid data. Mobile-first is a discipline, not a breakpoint. see [mobile-first design](/blog/mobile-first-web-design-namibia).

## On the relationship

**9. "Who exactly does the work?"** Names, roles, and whether they are employed or subcontracted. You are not hiring a brand; you are hiring hands.

**10. "What does the contract say happens when we disagree?"** Revision rounds, scope changes, and the exit: what you walk away with if the relationship ends mid-project. Read this clause before you need it.

**11. "How do we communicate, and on what rhythm?"** Weekly check-ins, defined feedback windows, a named person. Projects die of silence more often than of mistakes, a point worth internalizing from [how long websites take](/blog/how-long-does-a-website-take-to-build).

**12. "Why should I not hire you?"** The strongest question in the list. An honest studio answers it (wrong fit of size, budget, sector, or timeline) because a studio that wants every project takes bad ones. We answer it ourselves: businesses needing a page this week on a rented template are served faster elsewhere, and we say so.

## Reading the answers

The pattern to look for is specificity with edges: numbers, names, dates, refusals. The pattern to flee is accommodation, every question answered with enthusiasm and no detail. Vague yeses are how a project starts that later discovers it never had a scope.

One practical close: ask for one reference from a project that went sideways. Not the showcase client, the one where something went wrong and was repaired. How a studio talks about that project tells you more than the entire portfolio.
