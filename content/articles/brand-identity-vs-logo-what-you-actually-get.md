The most common confusion in brand work is also the most expensive: treating "logo" and "brand identity" as the same purchase. They are related the way a front door is related to a building. Here is the honest difference, and what each costs to do properly.

## What a logo is

A logo is a mark: the name styled, a symbol, or both. Done well, it is distinctive, legible at 16 pixels and on a vehicle door, and stable for a decade. That is a real craft with a real price, and a bounded one. You commission a logo, you receive files, you own a mark.

What a logo does not do: decide your colors beyond its own, say how your website is laid out, determine how you sound in a quotation or an apology, or keep six suppliers rendering your name consistently. A logo alone leaves every future decision unmade.

## What an identity is

An identity is the system of decisions that makes everything you produce recognizable without the logo being visible. It answers, in writing and in reusable assets:

- **Positioning**: what you are, for whom, against what alternative. One paragraph, argued and settled.
- **Verbal identity**: how you sound: vocabulary, tone by context, the sentences you would never write.
- **Visual system**: palette with usage rules, typography, spacing, imagery direction, and the logo's contexts and clear space.
- **Rules for the edges**: email signatures, quotation templates, social crops, signage: the places consistency quietly dies.

This is why our [brand work](/services) describes identity as a system, not a deliverable. The system is what lets a business look like itself at every touchpoint, and what lets a [design system](/blog/what-is-a-design-system-and-when-you-need-one) exist at all, because interface decisions need brand decisions above them.

## The working difference, in practice

A business with a logo only: every new piece is a fresh negotiation. The website designer guesses the palette, the signwriter guesses the spacing, the proposal template guesses the tone. The result drifts, and drift reads as smallness, customers cannot articulate it, but they can feel six suppliers disagreeing about who you are.

A business with an identity: new pieces are assembly, not invention. The rules were paid for once; every application after that is cheaper and faster and consistent. The identity compounds exactly the way a [design system](/blog/what-makes-a-design-system-scalable) does one level up.

The local proof is in the [cases](/cases): a group like [Mendozer](/cases/mendozer) needs the identity layer specifically because six businesses must read as one accountable structure, impossible to achieve with a mark alone.

## What each should cost and contain

Honest scoping, market-level:

- **A logo project**: research, concepts, refinement, files in every format, usage guidance for the mark itself. Bounded and quick.
- **An identity project**: positioning work, verbal direction, full visual system, the edge rules, and the guidelines document that makes it enforceable. Weeks, not days, because the decisions are the work.

The cheap versions remove the same things cheap websites remove: the thinking. Template logos are legal and sometimes fine for a first year; template identities are a contradiction in terms.

## Which one do you need?

If your business is pre-launch, one offer, one audience: a strong logo and a clear paragraph of positioning will carry you further than you think. If you maintain multiple touchpoints, sell to procurement, or run several business lines, the identity is the purchase, and the logo is simply its most visible file. When in doubt, start the conversation with positioning: it is the cheapest decision with the largest effect on everything after it, and the one [we start every engagement with](/contact).
