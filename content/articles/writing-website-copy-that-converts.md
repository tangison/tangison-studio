Most websites do not fail on design. They fail on words: pages that describe the business from the inside out, in the vocabulary the business uses about itself, leaving the visitor to do the translation alone. Copy that converts inverts the perspective, it starts from the question the reader arrived with, and answers it in the order they ask it.

## The visitor's three questions

Every page is judged against three questions, in seconds, in this order:

- **What is this?** The headline's whole job. Not clever, not atmospheric, descriptive. "Offroad fitment and accessories, Windhoek" converts; "Built for the wild" does not, because it answers nothing.
- **Is it for me?** The subheading and first paragraph's job. Who you serve, stated plainly, so the right reader leans in and the wrong one leaves quickly, both outcomes are conversions.
- **What do I do next?** One action per screen, phrased as the reader's move: "Get a quote," "Book a call," "See the work." The single most common copy failure is giving the visitor five equally-weighted things to do, which is the same as none.

## Specificity is the whole game

Vague: "We deliver quality solutions tailored to your needs." Specific: "We build websites in Windhoek in four to eight weeks, copy included, and we answer every enquiry within two working days."

Specificity converts for a mechanical reason: claims that anyone can make, no one believes; claims only you can make, everyone checks, and checking is engagement. The honest numbers in [what a website costs](/blog/how-much-does-a-website-cost-in-namibia) are better marketing than any adjective, which is why we publish them.

## Structure that respects the scanner

Nobody reads pages; they scan them and read the parts that catch. So:

- **Headings that carry meaning alone.** A visitor who reads only the H2s should still understand the argument. Our [redesign checklist](/blog/website-redesign-checklist-15-steps) is written this way on purpose, the bold leads carry the content; the paragraphs are the depth for those who want it.
- **Short paragraphs, one idea each.** Three sentences that make one point beat a paragraph that makes five.
- **Lists where lists belong.** Steps, inclusions, criteria, anything countable reads faster as a list and is what scanners are hunting for.

## The words themselves

Write in the reader's vocabulary, not the industry's. The test: would your newest customer use these words to describe their problem? [SEO](/blog/seo-basics-for-namibian-businesses) and conversion align here for a reason, the phrases customers actually type are the phrases that answer their own question when they arrive.

Cut hedges. "We can help you potentially streamline..." is three words of help and four of apology. Confidence is not loudness; it is the absence of hedging.

Never write claims you cannot stand behind. A fabricated testimonial or an invented statistic converts until the first customer checks it, and then it converts against you forever, a cost no redesign recovers.

## The mobile revision

Every page gets a phone revision: the headline still answers "what is this" at 390 pixels; the first screen still contains the action; the paragraphs still parse at a glance while standing in a queue, the constraints documented in [mobile-first design](/blog/mobile-first-web-design-namibia) apply hardest to words, because words are what the visitor reads first and last.

## The working method

Draft fast, cut hard, read aloud. If a sentence cannot be spoken naturally, it cannot be read naturally. Then the final test, borrowed from journalism: give the page to someone outside the business and ask them what we do, who it is for, and what they would do next. If they hesitate, the page is wrong, and no amount of design rescues copy the reader cannot act on.

Copy is not the decoration on the website. It is the website; the design is how it is delivered. Treat the words as the product, and the conversion metrics start behaving accordingly.
