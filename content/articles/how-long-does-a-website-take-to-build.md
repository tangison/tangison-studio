"How long will it take?" deserves a straighter answer than "it depends." The honest version: it depends on scope, but scope has typical shapes, and the delays that stretch projects are predictable enough to plan against. Here are the shapes.

## The honest tiers

**A focused small-business site — about two to four weeks of studio time.** Five to ten pages, professional design and copy, a contact flow, launch checks. The [Weca](/cases/weca) tier of work: one clear offer, one audience, done properly. The variable that moves this number most is not design; it is how quickly the client returns feedback.

**A structured business or organizational site — about four to eight weeks.** More pages, per-service depth, real content structure, integrations. This is where most of our [cases](/cases) live: enough surface area that [content structure](/blog/what-is-a-design-system-and-when-you-need-one) and navigation decisions carry their own weight.

**A platform or product — from eight weeks upward, honestly scoped.** Portals, dashboards, booking systems, anything with accounts or payments. The engineering is bounded; what makes these projects long is the decisions around the engineering, and pretending otherwise is how they get longer.

**The one-week website exists** — a single page or a tightly templated build. It answers the question "does this business exist online." It does not answer "why this business," which is the question every serious buyer asks. A week is a real option for a real need; it is a false economy for a business whose website is its main storefront.

## What actually eats the calendar

Three delays account for nearly every blown deadline, and none of them are code:

- **Copy arriving late.** The single largest cause. Design can proceed with placeholder text, but launch cannot. The fix is contractual: writing included in scope, or a firm copy deadline before design starts. We put the words in scope precisely to delete this delay — see [writing copy that converts](/blog/writing-website-copy-that-converts).
- **Decision latency.** Each round of feedback that waits a week costs a week. Two focused review rounds beat six scattered ones; the schedule survives clients who batch their thinking and suffers the ones who trickle.
- **Scope that grew quietly.** The fourth page becomes nine; the contact form becomes a portal. Not dishonesty — enthusiasm. The protection is the change conversation: anything new goes on a list and is priced before it is built, not discovered at the invoice.

## The studio-side schedule

For transparency, the phases and their honest proportions: discovery and definition take about a fifth of the calendar; design and copy together the largest share; build and quality assurance the remainder, with launch checks that are short but non-negotiable — the [redesign checklist](/blog/website-redesign-checklist-15-steps) items exist because each one has, somewhere, cost someone a launch.

Communication rhythm is the hidden variable: a weekly fifteen-minute check-in, on schedule, beats a monthly deep-dive that reschedules twice. Projects do not drift in months; they drift in silent weeks.

## The question behind the question

When a client asks "how long," the operational question is usually "when can I stop being embarrassed by the current site." Worth saying plainly: a staged launch beats a delayed perfect one. Ship the pages that carry the business the week they are ready; add depth in the weeks after. A live, honest, fast site outranks a launch date that keeps moving.

If the current site is the pressure, [start with the audit](/audit) — a written account of what is wrong, ranked, so the timeline and the fix are aimed at the actual problem rather than at the embarrassment.
